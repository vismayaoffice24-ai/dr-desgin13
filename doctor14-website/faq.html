Frequently Asked Questions

Clear, reassuring answers to common concerns about kidney numbers, creatinine levels, dietary restrictions, dialysis indications, and consultation preparation.

You should see a consultant nephrologist whenever routine blood tests show an elevated serum creatinine or an eGFR below 60 mL/min, if urine tests detect protein (microalbuminuria or proteinuria) or microscopic blood, or if you develop unexplained swelling in your ankles or face.

Additionally, patients with longstanding diabetes, difficult-to-control blood pressure requiring 3 or more medications, recurrent kidney stones, polycystic kidney family histories, or sudden drops in kidney function (AKI) benefit greatly from specialized nephrological management.

No, absolutely not. An elevated creatinine level (for instance 1.4 or 1.8 mg/dL) is a signal that your kidneys are under stress, but it does not mean dialysis is imminent.

Most patients with early or moderate chronic kidney disease (Stages 2, 3a, and 3b) who receive prompt clinical intervention, blood pressure optimization, modern nephroprotective medications (such as SGLT2 inhibitors and ARBs), and tailored dietary adjustments will maintain stable kidney function for decades without ever requiring dialysis.

Once nephrons undergo chronic fibrotic scarring (in established Chronic Kidney Disease), those scarred units cannot be regenerated. However, the progression of the disease can frequently be halted or dramatically slowed.

Furthermore, if the elevated creatinine is caused by an Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)—such as dehydration, an adverse medication reaction, or urinary obstruction—timely treatment can frequently restore kidney function completely back to baseline.

Every individual diagnosed with Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes or chronic hypertension should have their kidneys evaluated at least once a year if previous tests have been normal.

This annual evaluation must include both a Serum Creatinine test (to calculate eGFR) and a Urine Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio (uACR). If mild kidney disease is already present, Dr. Shyma Sunil recommends testing every 3 to 6 months to monitor therapy efficacy.

To make the most of your first consultation, please bring:

  • All previous blood tests (especially serum creatinine and urea over the past 1 to 5 years, which shows your kidney trend).
  • Recent urine routine and microalbumin (uACR) reports.
  • Any ultrasound, CT, or MRI scans of your abdomen or kidneys.
  • A complete, exact list of all medications you take daily, including blood pressure tablets, diabetes drugs, vitamins, and traditional herbal formulations.
  • Recent home blood pressure and blood sugar logs.

No. Common Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen, mefenamic acid, and diclofenac are directly toxic to compromised kidneys. They reduce blood flow into the glomeruli and can trigger acute kidney failure or accelerate chronic damage.

For mild aches or fever, paracetamol (acetaminophen) is generally much safer for kidneys when taken at standard recommended doses. Always verify with your nephrologist before taking any pain reliever.
