Renal Replacement Therapy

Dialysis: Types & What to Expect

Hearing that you need dialysis can feel overwhelming. However, dialysis is a life-sustaining therapy designed to clean your blood, restore your vitality, and allow you to continue living a meaningful life.

When Does a Patient Need Dialysis?

Dialysis is recommended when kidney function declines to Stage 5 (End-Stage Renal Disease, typically when eGFR falls below 10–12 mL/min) and the patient develops symptoms of uremia. These symptoms include persistent nausea, severe loss of appetite, uncontrollable fluid overload in the lungs, refractory high potassium, or acid-base imbalances that medications cannot stabilize.

Dialysis performs three vital jobs that failing kidneys can no longer carry out: removing metabolic wastes, eliminating excess fluid, and maintaining normal chemical and mineral balances.

Debunking the Myth: "Dialysis is the End of the Road"

Many patients fear dialysis because of misconceptions. In reality, modern dialysis treatments—supported by clean water purification, modern biocompatible dialyzers, and proper anemia control—enable thousands of patients to work, spend precious time with family, travel, and remain active for decades or while waiting for a kidney transplant.

The Two Main Types of Dialysis

Nephrologists tailor the choice of modality to each patient’s medical suitability, lifestyle, and home support:

1. Hemodialysis (HD)

Blood is pumped out of your body via a secure vascular access, circulated through a dialyzer machine (an artificial kidney) containing thousands of semi-permeable hollow fibers, purified, and returned to your body:

  • Frequency: Typically performed 3 times weekly, with each session lasting approximately 4 hours in a specialized dialysis center.
  • Vascular Access (The Lifeline): The safest access is an Arteriovenous (AV) Fistula, created surgically by connecting an artery to a vein in your forearm 2 to 3 months before starting dialysis. For urgent needs, temporary or tunneled neck catheters (Permcaths) may be utilized.
  • Clinical Advantages: Performed under direct supervision of trained dialysis nurses and nephrologists; no equipment needed at home.

2. Peritoneal Dialysis (PD)

Peritoneal dialysis uses the natural lining of your abdomen (the peritoneum) as the filtering membrane:

  • Catheter Placement: A soft, flexible silicone tube (Tenckhoff catheter) is placed permanently in your abdomen during a simple minor procedure.
  • How It Works: Special sterile dialysate fluid is instilled into the abdomen. Over several hours, toxins and extra fluid transfer into the solution, which is then drained and replaced.
  • Continuous Ambulatory PD (CAPD): Done manually, usually 3 to 4 exchanges per day without machinery.
  • Automated PD (APD): Uses an automated cycler machine to perform exchanges while you sleep at night.
  • Clinical Advantages: Greater freedom, no needle punctures, gentle continuous fluid removal, and independence from hospital visits.

Living Well on Dialysis

Thriving on dialysis requires partnership between patient, family, and nephrology team:

  • Adhere Strictly to Your Schedule: Never skip or shorten dialysis sessions; cumulative toxin clearance preserves long-term heart and brain health.
  • Manage Interdialytic Weight Gain: Track your fluid intake between sessions to avoid putting excess strain on your heart muscle.
  • Take Phosphate Binders with Meals: These medications bind dietary phosphorus in your gut before it can enter your bloodstream.
  • Protect Your Vascular Access: Keep your fistula arm clean, never allow blood pressure cuffs or blood draws on that arm, and check daily for the gentle buzzing pulse ("thrill").
