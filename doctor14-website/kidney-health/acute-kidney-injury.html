Critical Care Nephrology

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

Unlike chronic kidney disease which develops over years, Acute Kidney Injury is an abrupt decline in filtering power occurring within hours or days. The reassuring news: with rapid medical diagnosis, AKI can often be completely reversed.

How AKI Differs from Chronic Disease

In Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), nephrons are permanently scarred and replaced by fibrous tissue. In Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), by contrast, nephrons are usually acutely stunned or chemically insulted, but their underlying architecture remains viable.

If the underlying trigger—such as dehydration, severe infection, or a toxic drug—is removed promptly and the patient is supported clinically, the tubular epithelial cells can regenerate, restoring normal serum creatinine levels within weeks.

The Three Diagnostic Categories of AKI

Nephrologists classify AKI based on where the physiological disruption occurs: Prerenal (inadequate blood flow reaching kidneys), Intrinsic / Renal (direct cellular damage inside kidney tissue), or Postrenal (mechanical blockage preventing urine outflow).

Major Causes of Acute Kidney Injury

Identifying the exact category dictates immediate clinical resuscitation:

  • Prerenal Causes (60–70% of outpatient cases): Severe volume depletion from diarrhea, vomiting, burns, massive blood loss, heart failure, or excessive diuretic usage depriving the kidneys of renal perfusion pressure.
  • Intrinsic Renal Causes:
    • Nephrotoxic Drugs: Frequent use of NSAID painkillers (ibuprofen, diclofenac), certain antibiotics (aminoglycosides, colistin), or iodinated CT scan contrast dyes.
    • Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN): Prolonged severe lack of blood flow or septic shock causing shedding of tubular lining cells.
    • Acute Glomerulonephritis & Vasculitis: Rapid immune complex attacks on glomerular capillary walls.
  • Postrenal Causes: Severe urinary obstruction from an enlarged prostate gland in older men, large bilateral kidney stones, or pelvic tumors blocking both ureters.

Signs That Require Immediate Nephrology Care

AKI can develop during a hospital stay or following an acute illness at home:

  • A sharp, noticeable drop in daily urine output (oliguria: passing less than 400 mL in 24 hours, or no urine at all).
  • Rapid swelling in the ankles, feet, or eyelids accompanied by sudden weight gain.
  • Severe shortness of breath when lying flat, indicating fluid accumulation in the lungs (pulmonary edema).
  • Profound weakness, confusion, or severe nausea following an infection, dehydration episode, or a new medication.

Treatment & Recovery Management

Rapid specialist management aims to protect nephrons while they heal:

  • Hemodynamic Volume Restoration: Administering tailored intravenous fluids and electrolytes to re-establish robust renal capillary blood flow in prerenal failure.
  • Immediate Cessation of Nephrotoxins: Halting all offending pain medicines, stopping non-essential ACE inhibitors/ARBs temporarily, and adjusting remaining drug dosages to current eGFR.
  • Relieving Mechanical Blockages: Urgent urological stenting or urinary catheterization to relieve postrenal obstructions.
  • Temporary Supportive Dialysis: If dangerous potassium levels, severe acidosis, or fluid overload threaten the heart before the kidneys recover, short-term hemodialysis can safely bridge the patient for a few days to weeks until natural kidney function returns.
  • Convalescent Follow-Up: Patients who suffer severe AKI carry an increased lifetime risk of developing CKD later; regular 6-month checkups ensure complete renal recovery.
